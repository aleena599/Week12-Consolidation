# Week12-ConsolidationOut of everything that I have done in this course up to now, the most remarkable thing was redoing my digital workflow from the first few weeks of the course, more precisely, the week we tried Pelican and R for the first time. During the initial attempt, I was almost mechanically following the steps. But when I got authentic errors and saw the Discord community's way of dealing with them, I changed my approach, rather than just copying the instructions, I tried to understand the logic behind them. I focused on the reasons for the failure of things, not just on what the next line of code was supposed to be.
The transition from following to comprehending is, in my opinion, the most significant change.
            Technical difficulties persisted for people the same way they had for me. Pelican themes were not working due to the curl command, Windows users had to manually download files, an R installer was not clearly identified, and datasets were not being loaded because of column names being either missing or mismatched. I experienced the conversation and it made me want to fill the huge gap between reproduction and replication.
         When I was revisiting the material, it became obvious to me that a lot of students were facing the same technical problems as I did at first. The curl command would not work properly so the Pelican themes could not be downloaded. Windows users were to invent totally different workarounds like downloading files one by one. R installers were different according to the operating system and the exact version number mattered. Some datasets did not get loaded because their column names were not the same as the code; in some instances, the URLs had changed, the ADS interface had been upgraded or the files simply did not exist anymore.
             Furthermore, to answer the assignments question I think reproduction and replication are not the same thing, and we were all feeling that difference in real time.
               Reproducing something involves conducting an experiment in the same manner and expecting the same outcomes. It is similar to executing the exact same command that is stated in the laboratory manual and trusting that it will function the same way on your computer. Reproduction relies on the constant presence of stability, which includes stable links, stable versions, stable dependencies, and a stable environment.
            On the other hand, replication is entirely another story. Replication means to recreate the result by changing conditions, and modifying the method so that when the variables are shifted the output can still be achieved. The variables in our case included changing URLs that were not announced in advance, new R versions, columns missing or renamed in the datasets downloaded, Pelican themes not being available, and even having a completely redesigned ADS interface which was released during the course of the module.
             In the past, I thought that the workflow was a way of doing things that made things easier, but now I know for sure that the ability to replicate is not simply “better” but is rather indispensable. Digital archaeology turns to replicas for its very existence since it is not ever the same with the tools, environment, and data sources. These factors are always in flux and, therefore, our techniques must be robust enough to bear the instability.Digital archaeology is so much more than just using computer systems to deal with the data, it involves recognizing the technical, historical, and methodological circumstances that contribute to the various forms of data production, preservation, and access. Replication is a very important part of this epic field for quite a number of reasons:
Number one, The tools and the infrastructures are changing way too fast. Just like the ADS update demonstrated, a workflow that was effective one week might become completely useless the following week. The research would completely collapse if our methods were unable to accommodate the change.
          One of the above situations is already happening: columns disappear, metadata changes, websites go offline, and files may get corrupted. Coming up with replicas means one has to deal with that instability, and not just act as if it does not exist.
        Our output has to be understandable by different kinds of people. What I would understand as reproduction might work for me alone, in a very specific moment, on a very specific machine. But replication works for a classmate who uses Windows or a lecturer who works with Linux
Digital archaeology depends on the documentation, explanations, and methodology that is built up to cross over from one context to another.
                  The intricacy of the processes is not seen when everything "just works". However, the layers of technical decisions, assumptions, and dependencies become clear when the system fails. Replication teaches us to not only read the signals but also to decipher the machinery behind the signals.   My second run through the workflow compelled me to break down all these lessons. It was no longer a matter of completing the tas but, it was about actually grasping and taking in the tool ecosystem and the digital infrastructures.
          A comment I got at the beginning of the course was that I had to go deeper than just surface-level stuff and show a stronger involvement with method and meaning. Revising my workflow, taking into account the Discord conversations, made it possible for me to translate that feedback into action. For instance, I didn't just ignore the error messages, rather I questioned them.  I didn't just straight away think that the command would work, instead, I checked the file paths, URLs, and version numbers. I didn't just blindly use data, rather I gave attention to metadata and column structures. I didn't just passively follow the instructions, rather I documented the issues, the changes, and the adaptations. This is the kind of methodological awareness my instructor encouraged me to cultivate, and the redo became a space where I could apply that advice meaningfully.
         How My Thinking Has Evolved Since the end of Module 2, my thinking has shifted in many ways.  I now understand data as an artifact, not a given. Just like archaeological material, digital data has a history, it is created, cleaned, edited, reformatted, and sometimes damaged. Working with broken links or mismatched headers made me realize that data is not passive. It carries traces of the systems that created it.
To me error messages are the research process. Previously, an error was synonymous with failure. Now I consider it as evidence, hints showing the system's structure. Debugging is not one layer of an onion whereas excavation is another; they both are excavations. The community is an important factor that I consider to be community. Discord was not merely a support group but rather a shared interpretive zone. People gave different things such as code, screenshots, alternative downloads, and hypotheses. This collaborative troubleshooting is akin to the digital humanities work done in physical settings.
         My view of transparency and documentation has changed.  A method that is successful only once for a single person is nothing but a waste of time. Real digital scholarship needs documentation that allows replication across different platforms and eras. 
         Redoing the workflow also revealed new questions for me for example how do we maintain digital projects over time, especially when tools and platforms evolve?
Redesigning my original workflow not only required technical skills but also resulted in a moment of development of the whole research method. Transition from just copying what has been done to actually doing it and comprehending it denotes the extent to which I have progressed as a student on this course. Besides, the course has imparted to me the values of hardiness, flexibility among others, the very attributes that are required not only in digital archaeology but also in other areas where one has to manage delicate and constantly changing digital systems.
